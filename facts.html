Marker Decoder

Marker Decoder: Decoder Ring

In 1935, a kids' radio show started mailing decoder badges to its listeners. At the end of each show, kids used them to unscramble a secret message, usually a hint about the next episode.

Source: Crypto Museum · Suggest a correction

Marker Decoder: Code Breaker

In World War II, about 400 Navajo Marines sent battle messages in a code built on the Navajo language. Japan's own chief of intelligence later admitted his codebreakers never cracked it.

Source: National Archives · Suggest a correction

Marker Decoder: Intel Officer

In 1917, British codebreakers decoded a secret German telegram offering to help Mexico take back Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Once Americans read it, it helped push the United States into World War I.

Source: National Archives · Suggest a correction

Marker Decoder: Cryptographer

Elizebeth Friedman broke the secret radio codes of rum smugglers for the Coast Guard, more than 12,000 messages. Her work helped bring a million-dollar smuggling ring to trial in 1933.

Source: National Cryptologic Foundation · Suggest a correction

Marker Decoder: Enigma

In World War II, Germany sent secret messages on the Enigma machine, which scrambled letters a new way every day. Polish mathematicians cracked it first, then Alan Turing and the team at Bletchley Park, England, built machines called bombes to break the daily codes. Codebooks captured from a German U-boat in 1941 helped too. Some historians estimate this shortened the war by up to two years.

Source: National Cryptologic Foundation, Imperial War Museums · Suggest a correction

Big counts

Marker Seeker

Texas has more than 17,000 state historical markers, each one telling a true story about the place where it stands.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Good Listener

For Texas's 100th birthday in 1936, the state placed more than 1,100 markers and monuments. Many of those granite markers still stand along Texas roads today.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

County Collector

Texas has 254 counties. The biggest, Brewster County, is larger than Connecticut and Rhode Island put together, and the smallest, Rockwall County, is just 127 square miles.

Source: WorldAtlas · Suggest a correction

Museum Goer

About 1.8 million people visited the Houston Museum of Natural Science in 2023, making it one of the four most-visited museums in the country. Its dinosaur hall holds over 60 giant skeletons, including three T. rexes, and a three-story glass butterfly house.

Source: HMNS 2023 Annual Report · Suggest a correction

Landmark Seeker

Being named a Recorded Texas Historic Landmark is the highest honor Texas gives a historic building. The building must be at least 50 years old.

Source: Historic Fairmount · Suggest a correction

Ghost Hunter

About a dozen Texas markers go missing every year. Anyone who returns one can drop it off at a county courthouse, no questions asked, because markers belong to everyone.

Source: Texas Highways · Suggest a correction

Ghost Courthouse

Since 1999, Texas has helped counties fully restore 81 of their historic courthouses, often to look just as they did when they were new.

Source: Texas Highways · Suggest a correction

Texas Sampler

Every new Texas marker is made at a foundry in Indiana. A big one holds about 280 words, and about 185 new markers are approved each year.

Source: Texas Highways · Suggest a correction

Resting Places

The Texas State Cemetery in Austin opened in 1851. In 1910, Stephen F. Austin, the "Father of Texas," was moved there to rest among the state's heroes.

Source: Texas State Cemetery · Suggest a correction

School days

Scholar

The Texas Declaration of Independence complained that Mexico never set up public schools. Four years later, in 1840, Texas set aside land in every county to pay for them.

Source: Texas Education Agency · Suggest a correction

Dean's List

Rutersville College, chartered by the Republic of Texas, was the first college in Texas. Baylor, founded in 1845, is the oldest one still running today.

Source: Baylor University · Suggest a correction

Coast

Beachcomber

The Galveston hurricane of 1900 is the deadliest natural disaster in United States history. Galveston rebuilt and is still one of Texas's favorite beach towns.

Source: Texas A&M, Office of the State Climatologist · Suggest a correction

Island Hopper

Padre Island stretches 113 miles along the Gulf. Its national seashore is the longest undeveloped barrier island in the United States.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Lighthouse Keeper

Texas built 16 lighthouses along its coast. The Port Isabel Lighthouse, first lit in 1853, is the only one you can still visit today.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Gulf Coast Cruiser

Texas has 367 miles of Gulf beaches, plus more than 3,300 miles of shoreline around its bays.

Source: Texas General Land Office · Suggest a correction

Texas themes

Moonshot

When Apollo 11 landed on the Moon in 1969, Neil Armstrong's first message began with a Texas city: "Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed."

Source: National Geographic · Suggest a correction

Trail Hand

From 1866 to 1884, cowboys drove about 5 million Texas cattle north to market, mostly on the Chisholm Trail. Jesse Chisholm never knew the trail was named for him.

Source: National Cowboy Museum · Suggest a correction

Trail Boss

In 1866, Texas rancher Charles Goodnight turned an old Army wagon into the first chuck wagon, a kitchen on wheels for cowboys on long cattle drives.

Source: Buffalo Bill Center of the West · Suggest a correction

Roughneck

On January 10, 1901, the Spindletop well near Beaumont blew oil more than 100 feet into the air. It flowed for nine days before workers could stop it, starting the modern oil industry.

Source: Spindletop-Gladys City Boomtown Museum · Suggest a correction

Front Page

Gail Borden ran one of the first newspapers of the Texas Revolution, starting in 1835. Later he invented a way to make condensed milk, which fed soldiers in the Civil War.

Source: Texas Highways · Suggest a correction

Sweet Tooth

Imperial Sugar in Sugar Land traces its start to 1843, making it the oldest business still operating in Texas.

Source: East Texas History · Suggest a correction

Trail Master

The 10 Heritage Trail regions began as 10 scenic driving trails drawn by Governor John Connally in 1968, the year San Antonio hosted a world's fair.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Outlaws & Lawmen

Lockup

In many old Texas jails, the sheriff's family lived downstairs, and the sheriff's wife often cooked meals for the prisoners upstairs.

Source: Texas Highways · Suggest a correction

Outlaw Trail

Outlaw Sam Bass was caught in a shootout with lawmen in Round Rock in 1878. He died two days later, on his 27th birthday.

Source: Denton Record-Chronicle · Suggest a correction

Tin Star

The Texas Rangers began in 1823, when Stephen F. Austin called for men to protect early settlers. They are the oldest state law enforcement agency in North America.

Source: Texas Ranger Hall of Fame and Museum · Suggest a correction

Law and Order

The Texas prison in Huntsville, nicknamed "The Walls," was started in 1848. Its brick walls were 15 feet high and three feet thick.

Source: Texas State Library and Archives · Suggest a correction

Service

Fire Chief

Houston's first firefighters, starting in 1838, pulled a big barrel of water on two wheels to fires, with buckets hanging from hooks on its sides.

Source: Houston Fire Department · Suggest a correction

Healthcare Hero

The Texas Medical Center in Houston, organized in 1945, is the largest medical complex in the world.

Source: UTHealth Houston · Suggest a correction

History & heritage

Statesman

Sam Houston is the only person to serve as governor of two states and president of an independent country, the Republic of Texas.

Source: Sam Houston Memorial Museum · Suggest a correction

Hail to the Chief

Dwight D. Eisenhower, born in Denison in 1890, was the first U.S. president born in Texas. As a boy he sold tamales made from his mother's recipe.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Native Heritage

About 1,200 years ago, Caddo people built a village and earthen mounds near present-day Alto. The name "Texas" comes from the Caddo word for "friend."

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Secret Societies

The Grand Lodge of Texas was formed in Houston during the Republic of Texas. Its first leader, Anson Jones, later became the Republic's last president.

Source: Texas Escapes · Suggest a correction

Faith & remembrance

Holy Ground

Fifteen "painted churches" stand in Texas, mostly around Schulenburg. German and Czech immigrant painters covered their walls and ceilings in bright colors.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Mission Walk

In 2015, the five San Antonio missions, including the Alamo, became the first and only World Heritage Site in Texas, an honor shared with places like the Statue of Liberty.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

El Paso Mission Trail

Ysleta Mission, founded in 1682 with the Tigua people, is considered the first mission in what is now Texas. Its community is the oldest continuously lived-in settlement in the state.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Cathedral Crawl

San Fernando Cathedral in San Antonio, begun in 1738, is one of the oldest working cathedrals in the United States. The Alamo defenders' remains rest there.

Source: Aleteia · Suggest a correction

Many Paths

Founded in 1854, Houston's Congregation Beth Israel is the oldest Jewish congregation in Texas.

Source: Congregation Beth Israel · Suggest a correction

Never Forget

The National Museum of the Pacific War is in Fredericksburg because Admiral Chester Nimitz, who led the Pacific Fleet in World War II, grew up there in his grandfather's hotel.

Source: We Are The Mighty · Suggest a correction

Museums

Candy Quest

Dr Pepper was invented in 1885 at a drugstore in Waco. It is the oldest of the major soft drinks in America.

Source: Dr Pepper Museum · Suggest a correction

Black Gold

When the East Texas oil field was found in 1930, one downtown block in Kilgore had 24 oil wells pumping. People called it "The World's Richest Acre."

Source: Texas Highways · Suggest a correction

Rail Rider

Temple was born from the railroad. In 1881 the Santa Fe railroad sold lots at a barbecue to start a new junction town and named it for its chief engineer, Bernard Temple. Its old Santa Fe depot is now a railroad museum full of antique locomotives.

Source: City of Temple · Suggest a correction

Fort Patrol

From 1867 to 1885, Black soldiers known as Buffalo Soldiers guarded the West Texas frontier from Fort Davis.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Ranch Hand

The King Ranch, started in 1853, covers about 823,000 acres. That is bigger than the whole state of Rhode Island.

Source: Texas Monthly · Suggest a correction

Hall of Famer

The National Cowgirl Museum and Hall of Fame began in a library basement in Hereford in 1975. It now honors more than 240 women of the West in Fort Worth.

Source: National Cowgirl Museum · Suggest a correction

Heritage Trail regions

Brazos Trail Explorer

The Brazos is the longest river that flows entirely in Texas, about 938 miles. Its full Spanish name, Los Brazos de Dios, means "the arms of God."

Source: Brazos River Authority · Suggest a correction

Forest Trail Explorer

Big Thicket National Preserve became one of the first two national preserves in the country in 1974. Its bogs hold thousands of plants that eat insects.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Forts Trail Explorer

Fort Concho in San Angelo, built in 1867 from local limestone, is among the best-preserved Army forts of its time west of the Mississippi.

Source: National Trust for Historic Preservation · Suggest a correction

Hill Country Trail Explorer

President Lyndon B. Johnson spent about a quarter of his presidency at his ranch near Stonewall, which became known as the Texas White House.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Independence Trail Explorer

On March 2, 1836, Texans signed their Declaration of Independence in an unfinished wooden building at Washington-on-the-Brazos.

Source: Britannica · Suggest a correction

Lakes Trail Explorer

Caddo Lake is the only large natural lake in Texas. It formed when a giant logjam on the Red River, so solid that horses could be ridden across it, backed up the water.

Source: Texas Parks and Wildlife · Suggest a correction

Mountain Trail Explorer

Guadalupe Peak, at 8,751 feet, is the highest point in Texas. A shiny steel pyramid marks its top.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Pecos Trail Explorer

Judge Roy Bean called himself the "Law West of the Pecos" and held court in his own saloon in Langtry.

Source: Texas Highways · Suggest a correction

Plains Trail Explorer

Palo Duro Canyon is the second-largest canyon in the United States, about 120 miles long and 800 feet deep.

Source: KSAT · Suggest a correction

Tropical Trail Explorer

The first battle of the U.S.-Mexican War was fought at Palo Alto, near Brownsville, on May 8, 1846.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Seasonal hunts

12 Days of Christmas

Every Christmas season since 1947, a San Antonio group has performed Los Pastores, a shepherds' play sung in Spanish, at Mission San José. The play's roots go back to medieval Europe.

Source: National Park Service · Suggest a correction

Texas Independence Trail

The Battle of San Jacinto on April 21, 1836, won Texas its independence in about 18 minutes. The San Jacinto Monument is more than 12 feet taller than the Washington Monument.

Source: San Jacinto Museum · Suggest a correction

Memorial Day Weekend

Memorial Day began in 1868 as Decoration Day, when Americans decorated soldiers' graves with spring flowers. It became a national holiday on the last Monday in May in 1971.

Source: U.S. Navy · Suggest a correction

Summer Road Trip

Adrian, in the Texas Panhandle, is the halfway point of old Route 66, about 1,139 miles from both Chicago and Los Angeles.

Source: WFLA · Suggest a correction

Día de los Muertos

In 2008, UNESCO honored Día de los Muertos as a world cultural treasure. Families welcome back the memory of loved ones with flower petals, candles, and their favorite foods.

Source: UNESCO · Suggest a correction

Turkey Trot

On April 30, 1598, near present-day San Elizario, Spanish settlers and the Manso people shared a thanksgiving feast, 23 years before the Pilgrims' famous one.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Menorah Mania

Two Texas synagogues share an "oldest" title. B'nai Israel in Galveston, built in 1870, is the oldest synagogue building still standing, though it became a Masonic lodge in 1955. B'nai Abraham, built in Brenham in 1894 and moved to Austin in 2015, is the oldest still used as a synagogue.

Source: Texas Holocaust, Genocide, and Antisemitism Advisory Commission, Shalom Austin, Synagogues of the South · Suggest a correction

Passover Hunt

From 1907 to 1914, the Galveston Movement brought more than 9,000 Jewish immigrants escaping hard times in Europe through the Port of Galveston to new homes across America.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Holy Week Hunt

In 1847, when settlers and Comanches made peace near Fredericksburg, signal fires lit the hills. A mother told her children it was the Easter Bunny boiling eggs, and the town still lights Easter fires today.

Source: Texas Historical Commission · Suggest a correction

Ramadan Hunt

Houston's first mosque started in 1945 in a barbershop. In 1978, the city's first permanent mosque opened with help from boxing champion Muhammad Ali.

Source: Houston History Magazine · Suggest a correction
